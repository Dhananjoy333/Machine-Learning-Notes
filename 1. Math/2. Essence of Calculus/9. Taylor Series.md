Taylor series is mainly used to write a non-polynomial function in terms of polynomials function at certain point of interest. As polynomial functions are much easier to deal with , using Taylor series we will try to write some polynomial function that look similar in graph to non-polynomial function graph at some point.

![](assets/Taylor%20series/cos(x)-polynomial.svg)

Here we have a $\cos(x)$ graph and a polynomial $P(x)=c_{0}+c_{1}x+c_{2}x^2$ . Now we try to make this polynomial as similar as possible to $\cos(x)$ graph around the point 0.

 - First, at $x=0$ $\cos(0)=1$ ,so the polynomial should also give 1 at $x=0$ . Putting value of $x=0$ in polynomial we get $P(0)=c_{0}$ . So here $c_{0}$ must be equal to 1 for polynomial to be equal to 1 at $x=0$

![](assets/Taylor%20series/change%20c1%20and%20c2.svg)

- Now that we got $c_{0}$ value we can freely change $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$, but it wont change the fact that at $x=0$ both $\cos(x)$ and $P(x)$ give 1.
- It would be good if the derivative of both the function would also give same result. We know derivative of $\cos(x)$ is $-\sin(x)$ and at $x=0$ ,$-sin(0)=0$.

![](assets/Taylor%20series/c1%20val.svg)

- Here on polynomial side $c_{1}$ controls the value of derivative at $x=0$ we want derivative to equal 0. Hence $c_{2}$ must be 0.
- Now it would be better if the second derivative of $\cos(x)$ would also match second derivative of polynomial.

![](assets/Taylor%20series/c2%20val.svg)

- Here second derivative of $\cos(x)$ give -1 and polynomial give $2c_{2}$ ,for this to be -1 the value of $c_{2}$ must be $-\frac{1}{2}$ . Therefore

$$
	\begin{align}
	\text{At around x = 0,} \\
	&\cos(x) \approx 1+0x-\frac{1}{2}x^2  
	\end{align}
$$
>[! Note]
>1. $c_{0}$ was responsible for matching the output of $\cos(x)$.
>2. $c_{1}$ was responsible for matching first derivative of $\cos(x)$
>3. $c_{2}$ was responsible for matching second derivative of $\cos(x)$
>Here we can add more terms like $c_{3},c_{4},c_{5}\dots$ to match multiple other derivatives of $\cos(x)$
>For example,
>![](assets/Taylor%20series/c3%20val.svg)
>Here to match third derivative of $\cos(x)$ the value of $c_{3}$ must be 0.

![](assets/Taylor%20series/better%20approx.svg)

Here we can see, the more new terms we take in Taylor series the x-axis range at which $\cos(x)$ graph and $P(x)$ graph look same increases. Here fourth derivative of $\cos(x)$ give one and fourth derivative of $P(x)$ give $1.2.3.4.c_{4}=24.c_{4}$ to make it 1 $c_{4}$ must be $\frac{1}{24}$ .

If we notice the derivative of polynomial, all that is happening is the exponent of $x$ is getting multiplied serially i.e.,

$$
\begin{align}
    \frac{d^4(c_{4}x^4)}{dx^4} = 4.3.2.1.c_{4}\text{ or } {4!}.c_{4} \\
	\frac{d^6(c_{6}x^6)}{dx^6} = 6.5.4.3.2.1.c_{6}\text{ or } 6!.c_{6}
\end{align}
$$
And we need the magnitude of polynomial value to be 1. So $c_{n}$ value must be simply $\frac{1}{n!}$.

![](assets/Taylor%20series/val%20of%20derivative.svg)

Also the final value of higher order derivative is controlled by only one term at $x=0$. As lets say we do fourth derivative then all terms having exponent less than 4 ($c_{0},c_{1}x,c_{2}x^2,c_{3}x^3$) will become 0 due to they turning to constant value and derivative of constant is 0 and all terms which have exponent more than 4 ($c_{5}x^5,c_{6}x^6,c_{7}x^7\dots$) will have one $x$ remaining due to which when we replace $x=0$ all those terms become 0. Hence for higher order derivative only one term control the final result.

## Convergence Issue

When we add more and more terms to the  Tailor Polynomial we get increasingly closer to some specific value i.e., the series converges to that value. 

![](assets/Taylor%20series/converging%20to%20e.svg)

if we add more and more terms the total converges to the value of $e$ or the total becomes equal to value of $e$. And not just for $x=0$ or $x=1$ , for any value of x the more term we add all values start to converges

![](assets/Taylor%20series/converging%20to%20val.svg)

This is true for some other function like $\sin(x)$ and $\cos(x)$. But not for all there are some functions for which this convergence happen only within some range. For example 

A graph of $\ln(x)$ centered around 1

![](assets/Taylor%20series/ln(x)%20graph.svg)

Here when we see the graph of the polynomial 

![](assets/Taylor%20series/ln(x)%20polynomial.svg)

Here we see that at range 0 - 2 the polynomial does converges to the values of the graph of $\ln(x)$ . But outside the range

![](assets/Taylor%20series/ln(x)%20poly%20graph%20out%20range.svg)

Outside the range of 0 - 2 the polynomial graph just bounce up and down no matter how many terms we take it never converges to $\ln(x)$ graph values.

In such case when adding more terms does not converges to anything we say the series "diverges outside that range". 

And the range between the input that we are approximating near and point where points of polynomial do converge is called "***Radius of Convergence***".

![](assets/Taylor%20series/radius%20of%20convergence.svg)
