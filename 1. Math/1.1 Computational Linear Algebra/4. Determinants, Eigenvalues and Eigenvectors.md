The **determinant** (denoted as ($\det(A)$ or $|A|$) is a special scalar value computed from the elements of a square matrix. It indicates whether a matrix is invertible (i.e., non-zero) and represents how a linear transformation scales spatial areas or volumes.
$$
		\begin{align}
	\text{for 2*2 matrix,} \\
	A &= \begin{bmatrix}
	a&b\\
	c&d
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\det(A)&=a.d-c.b \\
	 \\
	 \text{for 3*3 matrix} \\
	 A&=\begin{bmatrix}
	 a_{1}&b_{1}&c_{1} \\
	 a_{2}&b_{2}&c_{2} \\
	 a_{3}&b_{3}&c_{3}
	 \end{bmatrix}  \\
	 \det(A)&=a_{1}(b_{2}.c_{3}-b_{3}.c_{2})-b_{1}(a_{2}.c_{3}-a_{3}.c_{2})+c_{1}(a_{2}.b_{3}-a_{3}.b_{2})
	\end{align}
$$
- If M and N are matrices whose determinant in non zero then
$$
	\det(M.N)=\det(M).\det(N)
$$
- If M is a matrix whose determinant is non-zero then
$$
	\det(M^{-1})=\frac{1}{\det(M)}
$$
## Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

When we do a linear transformation usually a vector is knocked off its span. But in some special transformation we have some vectors that stay on its span (i.e., does not change direction), this vectors only gets inverted, stretched or squished by some amount, this set of vectors are called eigenvectors and the amount by which it is stretched or squished is called their eigenvalues. Since on applying that special transformation on this eigenvectors it only results in stretching or squishing by some scalar amount we can represent the equation as :

$$
\begin{align}
A\vec{v}&=\lambda \vec{v} \\
	\text{here,} \\
	A&=\text{Matrix} \\
	\vec{v}&=\text{eigenvector} \\
	\lambda&=\text{eigenvalue of that eigenvectors}
\end{align}	
$$

Now that we got eigenvalues and eigen vector we can also verify the equation 
$$
	\begin{align}
	A \vec{v} &= \lambda \vec{v} \\
	\text{for } \lambda=2, \\
	\begin{bmatrix}
	4&2 \\
	1&3
	\end{bmatrix}.\begin{bmatrix}
	-1 \\
	1
	\end{bmatrix}&=2.\begin{bmatrix}

	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\begin{bmatrix}
	-4+2 \\
	-1+3
	\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}
	-2 \\
	2
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\begin{bmatrix}
	-2 \\
	2
	\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}
	-2 \\
	2
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	 \\
	\text{for }\lambda=5 \\
	\begin{bmatrix}
	4&2 \\
	1&3
	\end{bmatrix}.\begin{bmatrix}
	2 \\
	1
	\end{bmatrix}&=5.\begin{bmatrix}
	2 \\
	1
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\begin{bmatrix}
	8+2 \\
	2+3
	\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}
	10 \\
	5
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\begin{bmatrix}
	10 \\
	5
	\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}
	10 \\
	5
	\end{bmatrix}
	\end{align}
$$
Now if we have to apply linear transformation $A$ multiple times on the eigenvector $\vec{v}$ instead of doing matrix multiplication of $A$ multiple time we can simply 
$$
\begin{align}
	A^n.\vec{v}&=\lambda^n.\vec{v} \\
	\text{for example,} \\
	\begin{bmatrix}
	4&2 \\
	1&3
	\end{bmatrix}^{100}.\begin{bmatrix}
	-1 \\
	1
	\end{bmatrix}&= 2^{100}.\begin{bmatrix}
	-1 \\
	1
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\text{calculating }2^{100} \text{ is much easier.} \\
	 \\
	\text{Conditions :} \\
	&-\lambda\text{ must be eigen value of matrix }A \\
	&-\vec{v}\text{ must be eigenvector whose eigen value is } \lambda
\end{align}
$$
### What if $\vec{v}$ is not an eigenvector 

Here if $\vec{v}$ is not an eigenvector then the rule
$$
	A^n.\vec{v}=\lambda^n.\vec{v}
$$
is not applicable. For a random vector $w=2\hat{i} +3\hat{j}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\end{bmatrix}$ where $\hat{i}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}$ and $\hat{j}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}$ . If we apply transformation $A$ :
$$
\begin{align}
	A.\vec{w} &=A(2\hat{i}+3\hat{j})  \\
	&=2A \hat{i} +3A \hat{j}
\end{align}
$$
if $\vec{w}$ was represented with the help of eigenvectors ($\vec{v}_{1}$ and $\vec{v}_{2}$) instead of $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$
$$
	\begin{align}
	A.\vec{w} &= A(2\vec{v}_{1}+3\vec{v}_{2}) \\
	&=2A\vec{v}_{1}+3A\vec{v}_{2} \\
	\text{Now because }\vec{v}_{1} \text{ and }\vec{v}_{2} \text{ are eigenvectors we can repacle A with }\lambda \\
	A.\vec{w} &= 2\lambda_{1}\vec{v}_{1}+3\lambda_{2}\vec{v}_{2} \\ \\
	\end{align}
$$
So in order to calculate $A^n.\vec{w}$ where $\vec{w}$ is not a eigenvector and $\vec{w}$ is written in coordinate system of $\hat{i}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}$ and $\hat{j}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}$ . We move the vector to a new coordinate basis system where the basis vector are eigen values i.e., eigen basis.  
Taking the previous matrix $A=\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\1&3\end{bmatrix}$ where eigenvectors are $\vec{v}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}$ whose eigen value is $\lambda_{1}=2$ and $\vec{v}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}$ whose eigen value is $\lambda_{2}=5$. So we are doing a transformation which move from $\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ to a new basis of $P=\begin{bmatrix}-1&2\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$ . Now we can represent the linear transformation $A$ in new coordinate system as :
$$
	\begin{align}
	A &= PDP^{-1} \\
	\text{Where,} \\
	P&=\begin{bmatrix}
	-1&2 \\
	1&1
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	P^{-1}&=\begin{bmatrix}
	-\frac{1}{3}&\frac{2}{3} \\
	\frac{1}{3}&\frac{1}{3} 
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	D&=\begin{bmatrix}
	2&0 \\
	0&5
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	 \\
	\text{Now} \\
	A^3&=(PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})  \\
	&\text{ Here }P.P^{-1}=I \\
	A^3&=PD^3P^{-1} \\
	 \\
	\text{So, } \\
	A^n&=PD^nP^{-1} \\
	&=P\begin{bmatrix}
2^n&0 \\
0&5^n
	\end{bmatrix}P^{-1} \\
	 \\
	\text{Which is much easier to calculate}
	\end{align}
$$
Therefore,
$$
\begin{align}
	A^n.\vec{w} &= PD^nP^{-1}.\vec{w} \\
	here, \\
	P^{-1}.\vec{w}&=\text{represents the }\vec{w} \text{ in new coordinate system of eigen basis.} \\
	D&=\text{applies tranformation to the vector which result in same tranformation as }A. \\
	P&=\text{brings the vector from eigen basis coordinate system back to our original basis coordinate system.}
\end{align}
$$

### Why is A and D transformation similar?

We represent linear transformation as stretching or squishing of basis vectors which has same effect on all other spaces.
$$
	A=\begin{bmatrix}
	4&2 \\
	1&3
	\end{bmatrix}
$$
this simply represent that basis vector $\hat{i}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}$ moves to $\begin{bmatrix}4\\1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\hat{j}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}$ moves to $\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\end{bmatrix}$. To show a transformation we don't necessarily have to represent it with where $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$ moved we can take any two linearly independent vector in plane whose origin is (0,0) and show where it was and where it went and create a matrix out of it to represent the transformation. So we simply took two such vector where in one such vector $\vec{v}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}$ it simply gets stretched by an amount of 2 and another independent vector $\vec{v}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}$ which simply gets stretched by an amount of 5. So if we take vectors $\vec{v}_{1}$ and $\vec{v}_{2}$ as our basis vector to represent the transformation A we simply create a matrix that stretch $\vec{v}_{1}$ by 2 and $\vec{v}_{2}$ by 5 .
$$
	D = \begin{bmatrix}
	2&0 \\
	0&5
	\end{bmatrix}
$$
