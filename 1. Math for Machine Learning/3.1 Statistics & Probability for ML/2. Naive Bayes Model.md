Bayes Theorem is a fundamental Probability rule, for updating belief when new evidence comes. We compute Posterior probability from prior probability.

$$
\begin{align}
P(H|D)&=\frac{P(H\cap D)}{P(D)} \\ \\

&=\frac{P(H).P(D|H)}{P(D)}
\end{align}
$$

- It helps calculate posterior probability of Hypothesis H given data D.
- Naive Bayes is a ML Algorithm based on Bayes' Theorem. It is a classification algorithm.
- It is called Naive because it assumes features are independent.

Example : Spam classification for email containing words lottery and winning.
Let, 
- spam : emails that are spam.
- ham : emails that are not spam.

$$
	\begin{align}
	P(\text{spam | lottery }\cap \text{ winning})&=\frac{P(\text{spam }\cap \text{ lottery }\cap \text{ winning})}{P(\text{lottery }\cap \text{ winning})} \\
	 \\
	&=\frac{P(\text{spam}).P(\text{lottery } \cap \text{ winning | spam})}{P(\text{lottery }\cap \text{ winning})}
	\end{align}
$$

- Here for $P(\text{lottery } \cap \text{ winning | spam})$ means how many spam email has both words "lottery" and "winning" in it. Now we can easily find how many emails contains both word "winning" and "lottery". Also we know "lottery" and "winning" word have correlation as one word appearing increases the chances of other also appearing hence we can apply Conditional Probability Chain Rule :
$$
	P(\text{lottery } \cap \text{ winning | spam})=P(\text{lottery | spam}).P(\text{winning | spam, lottery})
$$
- Here $P(\text{lottery | spam})$ is asking in spam emails how many has word "lottery". And $P(\text{winning | spam, lottery})$ is asking how many spam emails that has word "lottery" also has word "winning". Now this is doable but if we increase the number of words that we need to search in spam emails lets say 10 words $A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}\dots ,A_{10}$. Our conditional chain rule become :

$$
	P(A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap A_{3}\dots \cap A_{10}\text{ | spam})=P(A_{1}\text{ | spam}).P(A_{2}\text{ | spam, }A_{1}).P(A_{3}\text{ | spam, }A_{2},A_{1})\dots P(A_{10}\text{ | spam },A_{9},A_{8},A_{7}\dots A_{1})
$$

- Now it is very unlikely that will find spam email that contain all then words in one spam email resulting in one of the values being 0. As the equation is in multiple, it results in entire eq. equating to 0. Resulting in $P(\text{spam | lottery }\cap \text{ winning})=0$ , so algorithm will show all emails as not spam. which is not what we want. This is called "***Data Sparsity Problem".
- To solve this we instead assume all words to be independent and for independent event we know :

$$
\begin{align}
P(A \cap B)&=P(A).P(B) \\ 
\text{hence} \\
P(\text{lottery }\cap \text{ winning | spam})&= P(\text{lottery | spam}).P(\text{winning | spam}) \\
\text{for 10 words} \\
P(A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap A_{3}\dots \cap A_{10}\text{ | spam})&=P(A_{1}\text{| spam}).P(A_{2}\text{| spam}).P(A_{3}\text{| spam})\dots P(A_{10}\text{| spam})
\end{align}
$$

- Now substituting in our equation we get :

$$
\begin{align} \\
P(\text{spam | lottery }\cap \text{ winning})
&=\frac{P(\text{spam}).P(\text{lottery } \cap \text{ winning | spam})}{P(\text{lottery }\cap \text{ winning})} \\
 \\
&=\frac{P(\text{spam}).P(\text{lottery | spam}).P(\text{winning | spam})}{P(\text{lottery }\cap \text{ winning})}
\end{align}
$$

- Now for for $P(\text{lottery }\cap \text{ winning})$ we can apply law of total Probability :
$$
	\begin{align}
	P(B) &= P(A\cap B)+P(\bar{A}\cap B) \\  \\
	&=P(A).P(B|A) +P(\bar{A}).P(B|\bar{A})\\
	 \\
	P(\text{lottery }\cap \text{ winning})&= P(\text{spam}).P(\text{lottery }\cap \text{ winning | spam})+P(\text{ham}).P(\text{lottery }\cap \text{ winning | ham}) \\
	
	\end{align}
$$

- Note :  The goal of the model is to compare values of $P(\text{spam | lottery }\cap \text{ winning})$ and $P(\text{ham | lottery }\cap \text{ winning})$. If we expand :

$$
\begin{align}
P(\text{spam | lottery }\cap \text{ winning})
&=\frac{P(\text{spam}).P(\text{lottery } \cap \text{ winning | spam})}{P(\text{lottery }\cap \text{ winning})} \\
& \\
P(\text{ham | lottery }\cap \text{ winning})
&=\frac{P(\text{ham}).P(\text{lottery } \cap \text{ winning | ham})}{P(\text{lottery }\cap \text{ winning})}
\end{align}
$$

- Here for both cases denominator is same so during the comparison, the denominator won't affect the result of comparison. For example :

$$
\begin{align}
P(\text{spam | lottery }\cap \text{ winning})
&=6 \\
 \\
P(\text{ham | lottery }\cap \text{ winning})
&=2 \\
& \\
P(\text{lottery }\cap \text{ winning})
&=10 \\
& \\
\text{Before division,} \\ \\
6 &>2\\
P(\text{spam | lottery }\cap \text{ winning}) &> P(\text{ham | lottery }\cap \text{ winning}) \\
 \\
\text{After division,} \\
 \\
0.6&>0.2 \\
P(\text{spam | lottery }\cap \text{ winning}) &> P(\text{ham | lottery }\cap \text{ winning})
\end{align}
$$

- No change in result of comparison. Hence, in Naive Bayes' Model we can ignore the denominator. 
- Next we simply compute which side the new email is more leaning towards spam or ham by :
$$
	\hat{y}=\text{arg }max_{y}\text{ P(y|lottery and winning)}
$$
***
## Advantage of Naive Bayes

- Easy to Implement.
- Effective when no. of features is large.

## Limitation

- Features are independent.
***
## Implementation Example 

### Step 1 : Training Dataset

| Email | Text                    | Class |
| ----- | ----------------------- | ----- |
| E1    | lottery winning now     | spam  |
| E2    | lottery prize claim     | spam  |
| E3    | meeting tomorrow office | ham   |
| E4    | project meeting today   | ham   |
### Step 2 : Calculate Prior Probability

- Number of emails : 4
- Number of spam emails : 2
- Number of ham emails : 2
Therefore,
- $P(\text{spam})=\frac{2}{4}=0.5$
- $P(\text{ham})=\frac{2}{4}=0.5$
So, prior belief is, any new email comes there is 50% chance of being spam or ham.

- today
Vocabulary size, $V=10$

### Step 5 : Laplace Smoothing

- Words like "lottery" and "meeting" never appear in ham emails . If an email comes with "lottery meeting" , $P(\text{lottery | ham})= 0$. So, when we check for 
$$
\begin{align}
P(\text{ham | lottery,meeting})&= P(\text{lottery | ham}).P(\text{meeting | ham}) \\
&=0*2 \\
&=0
\end{align}
$$
- This breaks our equations so we assume that all words that we are training on appears at least once in both spam and ham. So we add +1 to all words and updated word counts is :
Updated Spam emails word counts :

| word     | count |
| -------- | ----- |
| lottery  | 3     |
| winning  | 2     |
| now      | 2     |
| prize    | 2     |
| claim    | 2     |
| meeting  | 1     |
| tomorrow | 1     |
| project  | 1     |
| today    | 1     |
| office   | 1     |
Total words : $n(spam) + V=6+10=16$

Updated Ham emails word counts : 

| word     | count |
| -------- | ----- |
| lottery  | 1     |
| winning  | 1     |
| now      | 1     |
| prize    | 1     |
| claim    | 1     |
| meeting  | 3     |
| tomorrow | 2     |
| project  | 2     |
| today    | 2     |
| office   | 2     |
Total words : $n(ham)+V=6+10=16$

>[!Note]
>Laplace Smoothing
>$$
>	P(\text{word | spam})= \frac{\text{count(word) + 1}}{\text{total spam words + V}}
>$$

 - Ham Probabilities

	- Winning :

	$$
### Step 6 : Testing new Email

- Suppose a new email arrives : 'Lottery winning'
Spam Score : 

$$
\begin{align}
P(\text{spam | lottery, winning})&= P(\text{spam}).P(\text{lottery | spam}).P(\text{winning | spam}) \\
&=0.5*\left( \frac{3}{16} \right)*\left( \frac{2}{16} \right) \\
&=0.0117 \\
Score(spam)&=1.1\%
\end{align}
$$

Ham Score :

$$
\begin{align}
P(\text{ham | lottery, winning})&= P(\text{ham}).P(\text{lottery | ham}).P(\text{winning | ham}) \\
&=0.5*\left( \frac{1}{16} \right)*\left( \frac{1}{16} \right) \\
&=0.00195 \\
Score(ham)&=0.1\%
\end{align}
$$
Compare :
$$
	Score(Span) > Score(ham)
$$
Hence email is classified as SPAM.

>[!Note]
>Initially we have prior probability of 0.5 i.e., email is 50% spam or ham.
>Later we used Naive Bayes'  Algorithm to find posterior probability and find that email is more like to be SPAM.

## Edge Case 

- If the test letter is : "lottery winning huge". Here word huge was never trained i.e., we don't know $P(huge|spam)$ or $P(huge|ham)$ . In such case we can choose to ignore the word.
- In real dataset training is done with more than 10,000 unique words so such cases rarely happen.

Having a dataset with many unique words decrease this edge case possibility, and having a dataset with lesser unique words but distribution of frequently appearing words are skewed helps in modelling the algorithm better.
