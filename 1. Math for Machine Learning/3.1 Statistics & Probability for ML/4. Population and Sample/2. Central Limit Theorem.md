The **Central Limit Theorem** is one of the most important ideas in statistics. It explains why the **normal distribution** appears so often, even when the original data is not normally distributed.

The **Central Limit Theorem (CLT)** states that when you take independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random samples from any population with a defined mean ($\mu$) and finite variance ($\sigma^2$), the **distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution** as the sample size ($n$) grows—regardless of the shape of the underlying population distribution.

If we have any distribution of any form, as skewed as possible, and we take some sample of data from the distribution and find its mean, and again take same number of random sample and find mean if we keep repeating the process multiple times and plot the value of mean each time, the distribution formed will always be normal distribution and the average mean always approaches the mean value of initial population distribution.

For example,

Suppose you have a perfect uniform distribution from 0 to 10:

$$
	X\sim U(0,10)
$$
Its population mean,

$$
	\mu=\frac{0+10}{2}=5
$$
Now if we take some random sample $n=5$

$$
\begin{align}
	\text{Sample 1 }&=1,3,6,8,9 \\
	\text{mean }\bar{X}&=5.4 \\
	 \\
	\text{Sample 1 }&=0.5,2,4,5,7 \\
	\text{mean }\bar{X}&=3.7 \\
	 \\
	\text{Sample 1 }&=4,6,7,8,9 \\
	\text{mean }\bar{X}&=6.8
\end{align}
$$
If you take **thousands and thousands of samples**, the _average of all those sample means_ will approach the population mean :

$$
	\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu=5
$$
And as you increase the sample size from 5 → 30 → 100 → 1000, the **distribution of those sample means becomes increasingly concentrated around 5**.
