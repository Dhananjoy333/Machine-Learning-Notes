## Norms of Vectors

### L-1 Norm 

The L-1 norm is the sum of absolute values of components in a vector. It is also called Manhattan Distance.

![](assets/2.%20Vectors/L-1%20Norm.svg)

$$
\begin{align} 
\text{For } \mathbb{R^2} \\ \\

||X||_{1}&= |X_{1}| + |X_{2}| \\
 \\
\text{For above graph the L-1 Norm is,} \\
&=|3|+|4| \\
&=7
\end{align}
$$
### L-2 Norm 

The **L2 norm** (or Euclidean norm) measures the length or magnitude of a vector.

![](assets/2.%20Vectors/L-2%20Norm.svg)

$$
	\begin{align} 
\text{For } \mathbb{R^2} \\ \\

||X||_{2}&= \sqrt{x_{1}^2 +x_{2}^2+x_{3}^2\dots} \\
 \\
\text{For above graph the L-2 Norm is,} \\
&=\sqrt{ 3^2 + 4^2 } \\
&=5
\end{align}
$$

## Distance between Vectors

The distance between two vectors is the length of the vector formed by their difference. The most common method is the **Euclidean distance** (L-2 Norm), calculated as the square root of the sum of squared component differences.

![](assets/2.%20Vectors/distance%20bet%20vectors.svg)

$$
	\begin{align}
	\text{Vector A}&= (3,4) \\
	\text{Vector B}&=(-2,3) \\
	\text{Distance d between Vector A and Vector B,} \\
	d&=\sqrt{ (3-(-2))^2 +(4-3)^2 } \\
	&=\sqrt{ 5^2 + 1^2} \\
	&=\sqrt{ 25+1 } \\
	&=\sqrt{ 26 }
	\end{align}
$$
## Dot Product

The vector dot product (or scalar product) is an algebraic operation that takes two equal-length vectors and returns a single scalar number. It measures how much two vectors align or point in the same direction.

$$
	\begin{align}
	\text{Vector A} &= \begin{bmatrix}
	3\\4
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\text{Vector B} &= \begin{bmatrix}
	2\\3
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\text{Dot Product of Vector A and Vector B,} \\
	\vec{A}.\vec{B}&=\begin{bmatrix}
	3&4
	\end{bmatrix}.\begin{bmatrix}
	2\\3
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	&=3*2+4*3 \\
	&=6+12 \\
	&=18
	\end{align}
$$

With the help of dot product we can also find the angle between the vectors.

$$
\begin{align}
\text{Vector A} &= \begin{bmatrix}
	3\\4
	\end{bmatrix} \\ 
	|A|&=\sqrt{ 3^2+4^2 } \\
	&=5 \\
	
	\text{Vector B} &= \begin{bmatrix}
	2\\3
	\end{bmatrix} \\  
	|B| &=\sqrt{ 2^2+3^2 }\\
	&=\sqrt{ 13 } \\
	\text{Angle between Vector A and Vector B,} \\
	\vec{A}.\vec{B}&=|A|.|B|\cos\theta \\
	18&=5\sqrt{ 13 }\cos \theta \\
	\cos \theta&=\frac{18}{5\sqrt{ 13 }} \\
	\theta &=\cos^{-1}\left( \frac{18}{5\sqrt{ 13 }} \right) \\
	\text{In radian,} \\
	\theta &= 0.0555 \text{ rad} \\
	\text{In degree,} \\
	\theta &= 3.18^\circ \\
\end{align}
$$
## Multiplying Matrix with vector

$$
	\begin{bmatrix}
	a&b&c \\
	d&e&f \\
	g&h&i
	\end{bmatrix}
	*
	\begin{bmatrix}
	x\\y\\z
	\end{bmatrix}
	=\begin{bmatrix}
	a.x+b.y+c.z \\
	d.x+e.y+f.z \\ 
	g.x+h.y+i.z \\
	\end{bmatrix}
$$
Number of cols of matrix must be equal to number of col of vector.

$$
	\begin{bmatrix}
	a&b&c \\
	d&e&f \\
	g&h&i \\
	j&k&l \\
	m&n&o
	\end{bmatrix}
	*
	\begin{bmatrix}
	x\\y\\z
	\end{bmatrix}
$$
This is a solvable operation as number of cols of matrix equal to number of col of vector. Same rule for multiplication between matrices.

$$
	\begin{bmatrix}
	a&b\\
	d&e\\
	g&h
	\end{bmatrix}
	*
	\begin{bmatrix}
	x&y\\p&q
	\end{bmatrix}
	= \begin{bmatrix}
	a.x+b.p&a.y+b.q \\
	d.x+e.p&d.y+e.q \\
	g.x+h.p&g.y+h.q \\ 
	\end{bmatrix}
$$
## Determinant of Matrix

The **determinant** (denoted as ($\det(A)$ or $|A|$) is a special scalar value computed from the elements of a square matrix. It indicates whether a matrix is invertible (i.e., non-zero) and represents how a linear transformation scales spatial areas or volumes.

$$
	\begin{align}
	\text{for 2*2 matrix,} \\
	A &= \begin{bmatrix}
	a&b\\
	c&d
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\det(A)&=a.d-c.b \\
	 \\
	 \text{for 3*3 matrix} \\
	 A&=\begin{bmatrix}
	 a_{1}&b_{1}&c_{1} \\
	 a_{2}&b_{2}&c_{2} \\
	 a_{3}&b_{3}&c_{3}
	 \end{bmatrix}  \\
	 \det(A)&=a_{1}(b_{2}.c_{3}-b_{3}.c_{2})-b_{1}(a_{2}.c_{3}-a_{3}.c_{2})+c_{1}(a_{2}.b_{3}-a_{3}.b_{2})
	\end{align}
$$
## Inverse of Matrix

A matrix is invertible only if the determinant of the matrix is not zero. To find inverse of a matrix we use the knowledge of 
$$
\begin{align}
A^{-1}.A&=I \\
\text{Here,} \\
A&=\text{Matrix} \\
A^{-1}&=\text{Inverse of matrix A} \\
I&=\text{Identity matrix}
\end{align}
$$
Example :
$$
	\begin{align}
	\text{Let A} &= \begin{bmatrix}
	1&2 \\
	2&3
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	A^{-1}&=\begin{bmatrix}
	a_{1}&b_{1} \\
	a_{2} &b_{2}
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\text{Now,} \\
	A^{-1}.A&=I \\
	\begin{bmatrix}
	a_{1}&b_{1} \\
	a_{2} &b_{2}
	\end{bmatrix}.\begin{bmatrix}
	1&2 \\
	2&3
	\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}
	1&0 \\
	0&1
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\begin{bmatrix}
	a_{1}.1+b_{1}.2&a_{1}.2+b_{1}.3 \\
	a_{2}.1+b_{2}.2&a_{2}.2+b_{2}.3
	\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}
	1&0 \\
	0&1
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\begin{bmatrix}
	a_{1}+2b_{1} & 2a_{1}+3b_{1} \\
	a_{2}+2b_{2} & 2a_{2}+3b_{2}
	\end{bmatrix}&=\begin{bmatrix}
	1&0 \\
	0&1
	\end{bmatrix} \\ \\
	\text{Forming the equations,} \\
	a_{1}+2b_{1}&=1 \\
	2a_{1}+3b_{1}&=0 \\
	 \\
	a_{2}+2b_{2}&=0 \\
	2a_{2}+3b_{2}&=1 \\
	 \\
	\text{Solving the equations we get} \\
	a_{1}&=-3 \\
	b_{1}&=2 \\
	a_{2}&=2 \\
	b_{2}&=-1 \\
	 \\
	\text{Hence inverse matrix of A is,} \\
	A^{-1}&=\begin{bmatrix}
	-3&2 \\
	2&-1
	\end{bmatrix}
	\end{align}
$$
